# teardown-checklist.md

27 mechanical checks an agent walks per teardown, grouped into 5 categories. Every item is a yes/no question with a one-line note on what a yes means. This is the depth asset for Viral Post Teardown: the rubric tells you how to build the structure map and classify the hook, this checklist is what you actually run down the post to make sure nothing got skipped.

Version 1.0 | 2026-08-04

---

## How to use this file

Walk all 27 items on every post, in order. Answer yes, no, or n/a. A "yes" note tells you what to write into the teardown; a "no" is often a finding on its own (a missing beat, a flat rhythm, an unbacked claim) and belongs in the output, not in a discarded working note. Do not skip categories because the post looks simple. The checklist exists precisely because a skim misses the mechanical stuff a real read catches.

---

## Hook checks

**1. Does the hook fire within the first line of the post?**
Yes = the archetype classification is reading the real opening, not a buried one. No = note how many lines were skipped and why (see `rubric.md` Section 1's skip tests), because a hook buried under chrome or a CTA is itself worth flagging.

**2. Does the extracted hook line contain a CTA-mechanic phrase that should have been skipped (follow, comment, tag, drop a, save this)?**
Yes = the extraction was done wrong; re-run Section 1 and take the next line. No = extraction is clean.

**3. Does the hook match exactly one archetype on the precedence ladder, with no live competing match?**
Yes = the classification is clean and can be stated flatly. No = record the runner-up archetype and the one clause that decided the call, the way `example-run.md` does for the Number-Promise-flavored Story Cold Open.

**4. Does the hook contain a concrete number, date, or named specific, rather than a vague generality?**
Yes = the hook is grounded, which is a real feature worth naming in "why it worked." No = note that the hook works on tone or claim alone, which is a thinner kind of hook and worth saying so.

**5. Is the hook under approximately 120 characters?**
Yes = it is unlikely to need a second rendered line behind a "See more" fold. This is a working flag, not a platform fact, and the output never states an exact character limit as if it were one.

**6. Does the hook stand alone as its own paragraph, with a blank line after it?**
Yes = it gets to land before the reader commits to reading further. No = note that the hook shares its paragraph with build material, which changes how it reads on a feed.

## Structure checks

**7. Is there a clear Build beat between the hook and the Payoff: at least one paragraph of context, tension, or evidence?**
Yes = the post earns its payoff instead of jumping straight to the lesson. No = the hook and payoff are adjacent or the same sentence, which is its own kind of post (a punchline) and should be named as one.

**8. Does the Payoff deliver on the specific promise made in the hook, same subject, same stakes?**
Yes = the loop the hook opened is the loop that gets closed. No = name the mismatch specifically: what the hook promised versus what the payoff actually delivered.

**9. Is there a distinct reveal or turn, separate from the setup: a "what I did not expect," a reversal, a number that lands differently than predicted?**
Yes = the post has a turn, not just a report of events in order.

**10. Does the post state a general, reader-facing insight or lesson, separate from the specific story?**
Yes = it converts one person's anecdote into something the reader can apply, and this is usually the line most likely to be shared on its own. Quote it.

**11. Is that lesson line phrased as a reframe ("X is not Y, it is Z") rather than a flat instruction?**
Yes = it reads as insight the reader arrives at, not advice handed to them, and reframes tend to raise shares over comments because they are quotable as-is.

**12. Could the Build beat be cut in half without losing the Payoff's meaning?**
Yes = flag the post as loosely edited; the length is not earning its keep. No = the Build is doing necessary work, and that is worth confirming rather than assuming.

## Pacing checks

**13. Does the post mix short sentences (under 10 words) with longer ones (20-plus words), rather than holding one length throughout?**
Yes = the pacing is doing work. No = a flat rhythm reads as a report rather than a story, and that is a finding, not a non-finding.

**14. Are there 2 or more short sentences placed back to back anywhere in the post?**
Yes = name the exact sentences and their word counts; this is a deliberate tension-building device and one of the more reliable "why it worked" findings when it appears right before a reveal.

**15. Does each beat of the structure map (hook, each build step, payoff, CTA) get its own paragraph, separated by a line break?**
Yes = the post is built to be scanned on a phone screen, which is where nearly all of it gets read.

**16. Does any single paragraph run to 41 words or more?**
The boundary is stated as a hard count on purpose: 40 words is not flagged, 41 is. "Roughly 40" left the 40-word paragraph resolving one way in the checklist and another way in the output, which is exactly the kind of drift that makes two runs of the same method disagree on the same post. Yes = flag it by number and word count as a density risk. Check whether the density is earning its keep (carrying the post's key numbers or evidence) or just padding, and say which.

**17. Do the sentences carrying the post's numbers put those numbers early in the clause, rather than burying them mid-sentence?**
Yes = the numbers are doing hook or payoff work, not just supporting work, and are more likely to be the line a reader remembers.

## CTA checks

**18. Does the post end on an explicit ask: comment, share, tag, follow, or an equivalent platform mechanic word?**
Yes = name which mechanic it is asking for, verbatim.

**19. If there is no explicit ask, does the last line hand the reader a specific, personal completion task instead ("if you have a name in your head")?**
Yes = this is an implicit CTA, and it is the more common closing pattern in posts that read as personal writing rather than as an ad.

**20. Does the closing line address the reader directly, in second person?**
Yes = it is doing Direct-Address-style work in the close even though the hook may have used a different archetype entirely.

**21. Is the closing line short relative to the rest of the post, roughly under 15 words?**
Yes = a short close reads as an invitation. No = a long close tends to read as an explanation, and explanations at the end of a post are more likely to be skipped than read.

**22. Does the CTA repeat or reference the hook's specific stakes (the same event, the same number, the same subject), rather than a generic sign-off?**
Yes = the loop closes where it opened, which is a structural signal of a post that was shaped on purpose rather than written in one uninterrupted pass.

---

## Credibility checks

**23. Does the post include a specific, in-principle-checkable number (a percentage, a count, a dollar figure), rather than a vague quantity ("a lot," "most")?**
Yes = the number reads as evidence even without a cited source, because a specific number is a number that could turn out to be wrong, and stating it anyway carries weight a vague quantity does not.

**24. Does the post name a specific time or place anchor: a day of the week, a location, a duration in hours?**
Yes = it reads as a remembered, real event rather than a composed, generic example.

**25. Does the writer admit a cost, a mistake, or a downside anywhere in the post, rather than reading as an unbroken success story?**
Yes = it is more credible than a polished win-only account, and it is part of why Confession-flavored honesty in a post's Build beat tends to buy trust before the Payoff has to spend any.

**26. Is the post free of unverifiable superlatives ("the best," "everyone agrees," "guaranteed") that are not backed by a specific number in the same sentence?**
Yes = the post is not overclaiming, which protects the credibility of the numbers it does state elsewhere. No = name the unbacked superlative directly; it is a weak point in the teardown even when the rest of the post is strong.

**27. Does the post's central number appear consistently everywhere it is referenced, rather than shifting between mentions?**
Yes = the anchor number is stable, which reads as deliberate and checkable. No = flag the discrepancy by name and location; a number that changes between the hook and the payoff undercuts the exact credibility the specific numbers were building.

---

## Reading the results

A post does not need a "yes" on all 27 to work. Some of the strongest posts in this catalog's synthetic examples score low on structure (a Bold Claim post can skip the Build beat entirely and still land) and high on credibility instead. What the checklist is for is making sure every "why it worked" finding in the teardown is backed by a specific check that was actually run, not a general impression of the post. If a finding in the output cannot be traced back to one of these 27 items, it does not belong in the output.
