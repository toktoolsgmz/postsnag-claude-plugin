# hook-template-library.md

44 Facebook hook templates, organized by the 12 archetypes in `rubric.md`. Original patterns, written for this skill.

Version 1.0 | 2026-08-04

---

## How to use this file

Every template is a fill-in pattern with braced slots. Fill the slots with **your own subject matter**, taken from posts you have actually written. A filled template that would work unchanged for any creator in any niche is a filled template you should throw away.

**Keep the structural words.** The negation, the contrast, the punctuation, and the sentence rhythm are the pattern. The nouns are yours. If you rewrite the structure you have not used the template, you have written a different hook, which is fine, but do not expect it to behave like this one.

**Each template names the engagement it tends to earn:**

| Label | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Comment driver** | Asks for something the reader already has an answer to. Expect comments, fewer shares. |
| **Share driver** | Gives the reader something to send to one specific person. Expect shares and a lower comment ratio. |
| **Reaction driver** | Agreement that costs nothing. Expect reactions, low comments, low shares. |
| **Read driver** | Earns the click into the body. Engagement depends on what the body delivers. |

**You can check whether it worked.** PostSnag captures Reactions, Comments, and Shares as separate columns, so after you post, export again and compute `Comments / (Reactions + Comments + Shares)`. If you used a comment driver and the comment ratio did not move, the hook did not do the job you picked it for. That is a cleaner test than any feeling about a post.

## Question Open

Ends in a question mark. The reader answers in their head before deciding whether to read on, and the answer is what commits them.

**1.** `What would you {action} if {constraint that removes the usual excuse}?`
Use: forces a real answer by removing the escape hatch people normally use. Strongest when the constraint is a deadline or a physical limit.
**Comment driver.**

**2.** `How long does it actually take you to {small everyday task}?`
Use: the word "actually" is load-bearing. It signals you already know the honest answer is embarrassing.
**Comment driver.**

**3.** `Which is {comparative}: {option A}, or {option B}?`
Use: a two-option question gets more replies than an open one because the reader does not have to compose anything.
**Comment driver.**

**4.** `Is it {benign label}, or is it {uncomfortable label}?`
Use: reframes a neutral thing as a decision the reader has been avoiding. The second label is the whole post.
**Read driver**, and a comment driver when the second label stings.

**5.** `Do you actually {use the thing}, or do you just {what you really do with it}?`
Use: names the gap between stated purpose and real behavior. Works on any object people keep out of obligation.
**Comment driver.**

## Bold Claim

A flat declarative about how the world works. No hedge, no evidence in the first line. The confidence is the hook, so a bold claim with a soft body is worse than no bold claim.

**6.** `{Thing everyone buys} does not {job they bought it for}. {Thing nobody buys} does.`
Use: two short sentences, the second under five words. Earns its keep only if the body proves the swap.
**Share driver.** People send reframes to the person they have been arguing with.

**7.** `{Common practice} is the last step, not the first.`
Use: reorders something the reader believes is a starting point. Cheap to write, hard to argue with, easy to prove in the body.
**Reaction driver.**

**8.** `Most {systems, plans, routines} fail because they were designed on a good day.`
Use: explains a failure the reader has already had, without blaming them. Universally true and specifically applicable, which is the whole trick.
**Share driver.**

*(Template 9 moved to the Problem Callout section below: `You do not need more {resource}. You need fewer {decisions or inputs}.` classifies as Problem Callout under the ladder, not Bold Claim. See the note there.)*

## Curiosity Gap

Names a definite specific and withholds its identity. Precise vagueness, not general vagueness. The failure mode is a body that does not pay it off, and a reader remembers that.

**10.** `There is one {small specific thing} in every {place} that predicts {the whole outcome}.`
Use: the smaller and more mundane the thing, the harder the gap pulls. Name the thing in the second line, never later.
**Read driver.**

**11.** `The cheapest thing in my {kit, stack, process} does the most work, and it is not what you think.`
Use: works because the reader starts guessing immediately. Only use it if your answer is genuinely surprising, because a boring reveal costs you the next one.
**Read driver.**

**12.** `{N} things separate {the ones who succeed} from {the ones who do not} in {your field}. {N minus 1} of them are obvious.`
Use: pre-concedes most of the list so the reader reads for the one that is not obvious. Deliver the non-obvious one first.
**Read driver**, with strong shares when the non-obvious item lands.

**13.** `The hardest part of {process} is not the part anyone warns you about.`
Use: positions you as further along than the advice the reader has been given. Name the actual hard part immediately after.
**Comment driver.** People reply with theirs.

## Problem Callout

Second person plus a named failure. The reader recognizes themselves. Use sparingly: it is the easiest archetype to write and the fastest one to exhaust, because after the fifth diagnosis the reader wants a fix, not another mirror.

**14.** `Your {thing} is not {assumed problem}. It is {real problem}.`
Use: the workhorse. Reframes the reader's own diagnosis. Keep both sentences short.
**Reaction driver.**

**15.** `You keep buying {solution}. You still cannot {outcome}.`
Use: names a spending habit without insulting it. The second sentence must be the outcome they wanted, not a judgment.
**Reaction driver.**

**16.** `Your {thing} looks {good} in {favorable condition} and fails on a {unglamorous specific moment}.`
Use: the specific moment is the hook. "On a Tuesday night" beats "under pressure" every time.
**Share driver.**

**17.** `You {do the maintenance} every {interval} and it looks the same by {shortly after}.`
Use: describes a loop the reader is inside. Works best when your interval numbers are uncomfortably accurate.
**Comment driver.**

**18.** `You {redo the same task} every {interval}. That is not a {habit}, it is a {failure interval}.`
Use: renames something the reader was proud of. Slight sting, high recognition. Follow it immediately with the cause.
**Reaction driver.**

**9.** `You do not need more {resource}. You need fewer {decisions or inputs}.`
Use: the reader came for the first thing and you are selling the second. Only use when the body actually delivers the second.
**Reaction driver.**
*(Reclassified from Bold Claim: second-person subject in the first clause plus a state attributed to the reader is Problem Callout under rubric section 2.4, not Bold Claim, whose own first marker excludes a `you` subject. Number kept as 9, out of sequence with 14 to 18, so cross-references elsewhere in this catalog that cite "template 9" still resolve correctly.)*

## Number Promise

Leads with a count of what the post delivers. Bounds the read. The number must be the subject of the sentence, or it is not this archetype and it will not behave like it.

**19.** `{N} {things} I removed from my {area} that I have not missed once.`
Use: removal lists outperform addition lists, because addition costs the reader money and removal costs them nothing.
**Share driver.**

**20.** `The {N} {items} I tell every {client, reader, new person} to {action} on day one.`
Use: "day one" implies a system behind it. Keep the list to items that need no decision.
**Save and share driver.**

**21.** `{N} questions that end every "{the excuse people actually say}" argument.`
Use: the quoted excuse must be verbatim what people say, not a paraphrase. That quote is what makes it feel written for them.
**Share driver.**

**22.** `{N} minutes a day beats {N} hours on {weekend day}. Here is the math.`
Use: "here is the math" is a promise. Show actual arithmetic in the body or do not use this one.
**Read driver.**

## Story Cold Open

Drops the reader into a specific past event with no setup. A scene is harder to scroll past than a claim. Requires a real event; a manufactured one reads as manufactured and it is the archetype where readers can tell.

**23.** `I opened a {person}'s {place} last {weekday} and {specific absurd thing} {happened}.`
Use: the absurd specific does all the work. Name the object exactly. A sled, not "old sports equipment".
**Comment driver.**

**24.** `A {person} {emotional verb} in their own {mundane place} on {weekday}, and it was not about {the obvious subject}.`
Use: the "and it was not about" clause turns a story into a question. Answer it in the body, not in the hook.
**Share driver.** This is the highest-ceiling template in the library and the easiest to overuse.

**25.** `I spent {N hours} on {task} in {specific place} and left with {tiny result}.`
Use: the gap between effort and result is the hook. The body explains why the tiny result was the point.
**Read driver.**

**26.** `The first {project} I ever did, I did everything wrong.`
Use: buys credibility by spending it. Works best from someone who is visibly good at the thing now.
**Reaction driver.**

## Contrarian Take

Names a prevailing belief and rejects it. The explicit reference to what "everyone" says is what separates this from a Bold Claim, and it roughly doubles the argument the post generates. Use it when you can defend the position in the comments, because you will have to.

**27.** `Everyone tells you to {common first step}. That is exactly backwards, and it is why you own {evidence of the failure}.`
Use: the evidence clause is what stops it from being a hot take. Point at something in the reader's house or their file.
**Comment driver.**

**28.** `Unpopular opinion: most of us do not have a {named problem}. We have a {real problem} with a {named problem} symptom.`
Use: two words of prefix change the archetype. Strip "Unpopular opinion:" and this is a Bold Claim that performs differently.
**Comment driver.**

**29.** `The advice is "{common advice, verbatim}". I have watched {N} {people} follow it and {what actually happens}.`
Use: the count is the authority. Do not use a number you cannot defend.
**Share driver.**

**30.** `{Widely praised thing} is fine. It is also the reason {undesirable outcome}, and nobody says that part.`
Use: concedes first, which disarms the defenders before they arrive. "Nobody says that part" is the pull.
**Comment driver.**

## Direct Address

Names or filters an audience before making any claim. Everyone outside the segment scrolls, which is the point: a smaller, harder stop beats a wider, softer one.

**31.** `{Audience segment}, this one changes nothing about your {constraint they cannot change}.`
Use: leads with the objection the segment always raises. Removes it before they can.
**Save driver.**

**32.** `If you have {specific tell}, this one is for you.`
Use: the tell must be specific enough that people who have it feel caught. A labeled box, a certain browser tab, a particular drawer.
**Comment driver.** People confess the tell.

**33.** `To the person who {did the thing at an unreasonable hour} last night: I see you.`
Use: warmth, not advice. Do not attach a lesson in the first line; it breaks the tone.
**Reaction driver.**

**34.** `Anyone with {specific circumstance} and {specific consequence}, come here.`
Use: two conditions filter harder than one and make the stop feel personal.
**Read driver.**

## Confession

Present-tense admission that costs you authority. It is the archetype most creators avoid and it is frequently their best-performing one, because it is the only time they sound like the reader.

**35.** `I have a {area} in my own {domain} I do not {show or talk about}.`
Use: state it flatly. Any softening kills it. Describe the area honestly in the body, including the parts that are embarrassing.
**Comment driver.**

**36.** `I still {do the wrong thing} and I have been doing this for {N years}.`
Use: the years number is what makes it land. Without it this is just a complaint.
**Reaction driver.**

**37.** `I charge people for {expertise} and I {failed at the same thing myself} until {recent timeframe}.`
Use: the highest-risk template here. Only use it if the body shows what changed, or it reads as an apology.
**Share driver.**

## Command

A bare imperative as the first word. No persuasion. It lands hard or it gets ignored, and the median outcome is usually the second one, so save it for an action that takes under five minutes.

**38.** `Go {small action} right now. Set a timer for {small number} minutes.`
Use: the timer is what makes it possible to obey. Without it this is a nag.
**Comment driver**, if you ask people to report back.

**39.** `Stop {the visible activity}. Start {the invisible one that matters}.`
Use: two imperatives, under seven words total. Any longer and the rhythm is gone.
**Reaction driver.**

**40.** `Count the {things}. That number is your {diagnosis}.`
Use: converts an opinion into an arithmetic result the reader can produce in a minute.
**Comment driver.** People post their number.

## Myth Bust

Corrects a checkable belief. Different from a Contrarian Take: contrarian rejects advice, myth bust corrects a fact, and a myth bust needs proof in the body or it is just a louder opinion.

**41.** `{Product or method} does not fail because {reason people blame}. It fails because {actual reason}, and no amount of {money or effort} fixes {actual reason}.`
Use: the "no amount of" clause is what makes it useful, because it stops the reader from buying the more expensive version of the same mistake.
**Save and share driver.**

**42.** `"{Common belief, stated verbatim}" is not a rule. It is {the real origin of the belief}, repeated until it sounded like one.`
Use: needs a genuine origin story. If you do not know where the belief came from, use a Contrarian Take instead.
**Share driver.**

## Permission Grant

Releases the reader from an obligation. The rarest high-performing archetype, because it is the only one that reduces the reader's workload in the first line instead of adding to it. If you find this language in your post bodies but never in your hooks, you are sitting on the easiest test in this file.

**43.** `You are allowed to keep the {ugly or imperfect thing} that works. Nobody is coming to inspect your {domain}.`
Use: names the imagined judge and dismisses them. The second sentence is the release; do not cut it.
**Share driver.** Readers send this to someone who needs it.

**44.** `You do not have to {the obligation everyone assumes}. {N} of my {clients or readers} never did, and {outcome}.`
Use: the count converts permission into evidence, which is what makes it survive the reader's skepticism.
**Reaction driver.**

## Two things this library will not do for you

**It will not tell you which template is best.** That depends on your audience, and the whole point of the hook analyzer is that your file already knows. Run the analysis, find the archetypes where your share of the top quartile beats your share of the corpus, then come here and pull templates from those archetypes.

**It will not survive being used as a form.** Fill a template with someone else's subject matter and it reads like a template, which is the one thing a hook cannot afford to do. Fill it with a specific object from a specific post you actually wrote, and it disappears into your voice.
