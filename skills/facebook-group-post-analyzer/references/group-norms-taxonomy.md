# group-norms-taxonomy.md

24 norms that show up across Facebook groups generally, not scoped to any single captured group. `rubric.md` sections 3 and 4 measure four of these (length, first-person tone, question-mark tone, promo tolerance) on the group you actually captured. This file is the wider map: what else to look for, how to spot it in a PostSnag group export using only the 9 columns that export carries, and what respecting versus violating each norm looks like. Original writing, built for this skill.

Version 1.0 | 2026-08-04

---

## How to use this file

Every norm below names an **observable signal**, meaning something you can actually check in `Author, Username, Posted, Type, Reactions, Comments, Shares, Content, Post URL`, because that is the entire group export and nothing outside it exists to check. A norm you cannot measure from those 9 columns does not belong in this skill's output, no matter how real it feels from reading the posts. Where a norm needs judgment beyond a mechanical marker, this file says so rather than pretending a keyword match settles it.

Run `rubric.md` sections 3 and 4 first, on the group you captured. Come here afterward if you want to check a group for a norm those sections do not already cover.

---

### 1. Length ceiling

**Observable signal.** A sharp drop-off in `Content` word counts above some point, rather than a smooth taper. **Measure.** Sort word counts descending and look for a gap, not just a maximum. **Respecting vs violating.** A post that stays under the ceiling reads as normal length for the room. One that runs far past it, with no comparable neighbor, is testing a limit whether the poster meant to or not. **Example.** A group where every post but one sits under 60 words, and the one exception, at 400 words, sits at the bottom of the engagement table: the ceiling is real even though nobody wrote it down.

### 2. Visual-first culture

**Observable signal.** `Type` distribution skewed heavily toward `photo`, `video`, or `album`, with `text` a small minority. **Measure.** Type share of the whole corpus. **Respecting vs violating.** Pairing even a short caption with an image matches the room. A long `text` post with no image in a visual-first group is swimming upstream. **Example.** A renovation group where 80 percent of posts carry a `photo` or `video` type: a text-only progress update in that room is competing against a format the group was not built around.

### 3. Self-promotion tolerance

**Observable signal.** Language markers for a pitch, a discount, a booking call to action, or an affiliate link, measured directly in `rubric.md` section 3.3. **Measure.** The promo-marker heuristic in that section, applied to the group you captured. **Respecting vs violating.** Groups vary enormously here: some tolerate an occasional service mention, others treat any pitch as spam regardless of phrasing. **Example.** The same "3 spots open this month" line reads as normal in a group built around a service provider's own community and as a violation in a general interest group with no commercial framing anywhere else in the file.

### 4. Recurring thread loyalty

**Observable signal.** A repeated opening phrase or structure appearing across multiple posts over time, sometimes from one author, sometimes adopted by several. **Measure.** Scan the first line of every post for near-duplicate openings across different `Posted` dates. **Respecting vs violating.** Posting into the existing format (a "check in" thread, a "win of the week" thread) rides an established groove. Posting a similar idea under a new, unmatched title competes against the format instead of using it. **Example.** A weekly "Friday check in" thread that recurs with the same two words at the top of the post, every week, from the same or different authors: matching that opening is free credibility a new title does not carry.

### 5. First-person vulnerability tone

**Observable signal.** First-person pronouns present anywhere in `Content`, measured in `rubric.md` section 3.2. **Measure.** The presence test in that section. **Respecting vs violating.** A group that runs 70+ percent first-person rewards an admission or a personal account over a general statement. **Example.** "I still have not touched the garage" earns a different response than "The garage is often the hardest room," even when the two sentences make roughly the same point.

### 6. Direct-question comment bait, gated by trust

**Observable signal.** A question-mark post's engagement concentrated in one or two authors rather than spread across the bucket, caught by the concentration check in `rubric.md` section 3.2. **Measure.** Run the concentration check on the question-mark bucket before calling questions a norm; if it fails, check whether the same author supplies most of the top performers. **Respecting vs violating.** In a group where this fires, a stranger's question post does not inherit the effect. It belongs to the poster's standing in the room, not to the punctuation. **Example.** The shared synthetic file is exactly this case: question marks look like a norm until the concentration check shows two of the five question posts, both from the same recurring author, are carrying the entire bucket.

### 7. Before-and-after documentation

**Observable signal.** `Content` describing a starting state and an end state in the same post, often with a time or effort marker between them ("took forty minutes," "after two hours"). **Measure.** Keyword scan for `before`, `after`, and effort or duration phrases, cross-checked against `Type` (usually `photo` or `video`). **Respecting vs violating.** Showing the process, not just the result, matches groups built around transformation. A result-only post with no visible effort can read as a flex rather than a contribution. **Example.** "Pantry restock day. Everything decanted and labeled" reads differently depending on whether the caption also names what it replaced.

### 8. Newcomer intro ritual

**Observable signal.** A post's opening line matching an introduction pattern: "New here," "First post," "Hi all, I'm new," often followed by a brief self-description. **Measure.** Keyword match on the first line of `Content`, cross-checked against whether that `Author` has no other posts in the capture. **Respecting vs violating.** Groups with a visible intro ritual tend to welcome it warmly (a comment-heavy response even at modest reach). Skipping straight to a request for help, with no intro, can read as presumptuous in groups where the ritual is strong. **Example.** A group where every first-time poster in the capture opens the same way is telling you the ritual is load-bearing, not optional.

### 9. Location tagging

**Observable signal.** A city, neighborhood, or ZIP-adjacent term inside `Content`. **Measure.** This one needs judgment, not a fixed keyword list, because place names vary infinitely. Read a sample of posts for location mentions rather than trying to automate this one. **Respecting vs violating.** Local and buy-sell groups often expect a location in every post; general interest groups usually do not. **Example.** A regional group where posts naming a specific neighborhood get replies from people nearby, and posts with no location get generic reactions only.

### 10. Admin or moderator tagging etiquette

**Observable signal.** `Content` addressing moderators directly, often near a promotional or borderline post. **Measure.** Keyword match on `mod`, `mods`, `admin`, or an `@` mention paired with one of those words. **Respecting vs violating.** Flagging your own borderline post to the mods before they have to is a courtesy some groups reward with tolerance. Silence on a borderline post can read as trying to sneak it past the rules. **Example.** The shared synthetic file has a real instance of this: Author D's affiliate post closes with "Mods, let me know if this is not allowed," and it is also the single lowest-engagement post in the capture, which is worth noting as its own small finding rather than assuming the disclosure line helped.

### 11. Numbered-rule citation

**Observable signal.** `Content` referencing the group's own posted rules by number or by name ("per rule 3," "self-promo Sundays only"). **Measure.** Keyword match on `rule`, a number, and a group-specific term if one recurs. **Respecting vs violating.** Citing the rule you are following signals you read them. A post that would obviously violate a commonly-numbered rule (no selling, no outside links) with no acknowledgment reads as either not reading the rules or hoping nobody checks. **Example.** "Following rule 4, posting my swap list on the designated day" versus an unrelated post carrying a price and a swap offer with no framing at all.

### 12. Posting cadence tolerance per author

**Observable signal.** Gaps between the same author's consecutive `Posted` timestamps, and whether closely spaced posts from one author underperform relative to that author's own median. **Measure.** For any author with `n >= 3` (the same floor as `rubric.md` section 4), compute days between consecutive posts and compare engagement on tightly spaced pairs against the author's own baseline. **Respecting vs violating.** Some groups tolerate a poster showing up several times a week; others visibly fatigue on a second post from the same person within days. **Example.** An author's third post in five days landing well below their first two, all else equal, is a cadence signal worth naming even though it is not a formal ranked bucket.

### 13. Emoji density

**Observable signal.** Count of emoji characters in `Content`, as a rate per 100 words rather than a raw count so posts of different lengths compare fairly. **Measure.** Count emoji-range characters, divide by word count, multiply by 100. **Respecting vs violating.** A group running heavy emoji density where a post arrives with zero can read as flat or overly formal. The reverse is also true: a low-emoji group where a post arrives dense with them can read as noise. **Example.** Two groups on the same topic can have opposite norms here, which is exactly why this is measured per capture, never assumed from the niche.

### 14. Hashtag absence

**Observable signal.** `#word` tokens inside `Content`. **Measure.** Simple regex count of hashtag tokens. **Respecting vs violating.** Most Facebook groups carry near-zero hashtag use, unlike Instagram or TikTok. A post arriving with a hashtag block in a hashtag-free group reads as cross-posted from another platform without adjustment, which some groups tolerate and some visibly do not. **Example.** A caption ending in six stacked hashtags, in a group where nothing else in the capture carries one, is worth flagging as an import artifact.

### 15. External link tolerance, beyond promotion

**Observable signal.** `Type = link` posts and their median engagement against the group median, independent of whether they are also promo-marked. **Measure.** Same bucketing as `rubric.md` section 2, read specifically for the non-promotional link posts once the promo overlap in that section has been separated out. **Respecting vs violating.** Some groups welcome an outside article or resource; others treat any off-platform link as a reason to scroll past. **Example.** A group where the only two link posts in the capture are both promotional (the shared synthetic case) cannot answer this question at all. It needs a capture with a non-promotional link post in it before this norm is measurable.

### 16. Milestone and celebration posts

**Observable signal.** `Content` naming a duration or count alongside celebratory language ("one year," "finally," "after six months"). **Measure.** Keyword scan for duration phrases plus a positive-outcome marker. **Respecting vs violating.** Groups that celebrate milestones warmly tend to show strong comment ratios (`Comments / engagement`, from `references/foundations.md` section 1) on these posts specifically, discussion rather than passive reactions. **Example.** "Six months after the shopping problem post, I have bought two things for the house since January" is a milestone callback, and whether it earns comments or just reactions tells you how the group treats this format.

### 17. Venting and negative-post tolerance

**Observable signal.** `Content` expressing frustration or failure with no stated fix or next step. **Measure.** Keyword scan for frustration language ("nothing works," "I give up," "so over this") not followed by an action or resolution in the same post. **Respecting vs violating.** Some groups are built for exactly this and reward raw venting with high comment counts. Others expect every post to land on an action or a question, and an unresolved vent underperforms. **Example.** A pure vent post's `comment_ratio` against the group's typical comment ratio tells you which kind of room this is.

### 18. Advice-request versus advice-delivered balance

**Observable signal.** The share of posts phrased as a request for help versus a declarative account of what the author did. **Measure.** Cross-reference the question-mark marker from `rubric.md` section 3.2 with whether the question is actually a request for input, not a rhetorical check-in question. **Respecting vs violating.** A group dominated by requests for advice can read a declarative "here is how I did it" post as generous and welcome, or as showing off, depending on tone markers covered elsewhere in this file. **Example.** Compare the median engagement of clearly-asking posts against clearly-showing posts, separately from the general question-mark bucket, since a rhetorical "who else struggles with this?" is not the same speech act as "can anyone help me fix this."

### 19. Cross-post and repost tolerance

**Observable signal.** Near-duplicate `Content` text appearing more than once in the capture, from the same or different `Author` values. **Measure.** A simple text-similarity check between posts (shared long substrings or near-identical opening and closing lines) is enough; this does not need anything more sophisticated. **Respecting vs violating.** Some groups tolerate a popular post being reshared or reposted by request; others visibly penalize repeated content with falling engagement on the second appearance. **Example.** If the same before-and-after description appears twice, three weeks apart, from the same author, compare the two engagement numbers directly, that comparison is cleaner evidence than anything inferred from a single post.

### 20. Selling and swap listing format

**Observable signal.** Price markers, condition language, or trade offers inside `Content` ("$20," "OBO," "trade for"). **Measure.** Keyword and symbol scan for price and condition language, most relevant in groups explicitly built around swapping or selling. **Respecting vs violating.** A listing that includes price, condition, and a clear ask matches the expected shape. A bare "message me" with no details is asking the room to do the work a proper listing would have done itself. **Example.** In a swap-oriented group, compare engagement on listings that state a condition against listings that do not; that comparison is the whole norm.

### 21. Weekly theme-day culture

**Observable signal.** A title pattern repeating on the same day of the week ("Motivation Monday," "Win-list Wednesday"), connecting directly to the day-of-week layer in `rubric.md` section 5. **Measure.** Cross-reference recurring opening phrases (norm 4 above) against the `Posted` day of week. **Respecting vs violating.** Matching a named theme day, when one exists, borrows the day's own established audience. Posting an unrelated topic on a day with a strong theme can get buried under posts that do match it. **Example.** If the day-of-week layer in section 5 turns up a rankable day, check whether that day also happens to carry a recurring title pattern before crediting the day alone. That is the same discipline the format-confound check applies to post type, aimed at a different variable.

### 22. Comment-versus-reaction balance

**Observable signal.** The group's overall `comment_ratio`, `Comments / (Reactions + Comments + Shares)`, per `references/foundations.md` section 1. **Measure.** Compute the corpus-wide comment ratio and compare individual posts against it. **Respecting vs violating.** A group with a high baseline comment ratio is a discussion room: it rewards posts that invite a reply, not just a click of a reaction button. A group with a low ratio and high reaction counts is a broadcast room: strong content gets acknowledged, not discussed. **Example.** Two groups can carry the same median engagement number and be completely different rooms once the comment ratio is checked separately, one built for conversation, one for approval.

### 23. Screenshot and proof-of-work culture

**Observable signal.** `Content` referencing a screenshot, a receipt, a before number, or other evidence of the claim being made, usually paired with `Type = photo`. **Measure.** Keyword scan for `screenshot`, `receipt`, `proof`, or a specific number presented as evidence, cross-referenced with `Type`. **Respecting vs violating.** Groups built around measurable progress (weight, savings, decluttered items) often expect the evidence, not just the claim. A results claim with no visual backing can read as unverifiable in that kind of room. **Example.** "14 items expired in 2023" paired with a photo of the actual freezer contents lands differently than the same sentence with no image at all.

### 24. Anonymous or admin-relayed posting

**Observable signal.** `Author` showing a generic value like "Group Admin" or `Content` opening with a relay phrase ("Posting for someone who wants to stay anonymous"). **Measure.** Check whether any `Author` value in the capture is a role name rather than a person's name, and scan openings for relay phrasing. **Respecting vs violating.** Groups that offer an anonymous-posting feature usually have a visible convention for how the relayed post is framed. A post using that convention gets read as legitimate; one that looks like a personal post but is clearly relayed without saying so can read as evasive once noticed. **Example.** If a capture contains even one admin-relayed post, note the convention it uses so a user posting through the same feature can match it rather than guess.

## What this taxonomy does not do

It does not replace `rubric.md` sections 3 and 4, which are the four norms this skill actually measures and reports on every run. It does not claim any of these 24 apply to every group, or to the group you captured specifically, without you checking the signal against that group's own export. And it does not invent a marker list for every norm the way section 3.3 does for promotion: several of these (location, cross-posting, cadence) are named as things worth a human read, not as something a keyword match settles on its own. A taxonomy that pretended otherwise would be less honest than one that says, plainly, where the mechanical check ends and a human reading the posts has to take over.
